Your two exams

Paper 1The study of religions: Christianity and Islam. 1 hour 45 minutes. About 50 minutes on each religion.
Paper 2AThematic studies: you answer four themes. At HAP: A, D, E and F. 1 hour 45 minutes: 25 minutes per theme.
50% eachEach paper is worth half your GCSE. Bring a black pen.

Each section has five questions

From summer 2026 every section follows this pattern: 1 + 1 + 4 + 6 + 12 = 24 marks, plus 3 marks for spelling, punctuation and grammar. Paper 1 has a Beliefs and a Practices section for each religion. Paper 2A has one section per theme.

1mark
Multiple choice

Choose the one correct answer from four.

Read all four options before you choose. Never leave it blank.

1mark
Give one…

Write one short, accurate point. A few words or one sentence.

Don’t explain: just state it. Example: Give one religious belief about the nature of the family. “Children should honour their parents.”

4marks
Explain two (different / contrasting) …

Two points. Each point is stated (1 mark) then developed (1 mark).

Use “This means that…” or “This is because…” to develop each point. In the themes, ‘contrasting’ questions need the main religious tradition in Britain (Christianity) and one or more other traditions, e.g. Catholic vs Church of England, or Christian vs Muslim.

6marks
Explain two religious beliefs… Refer to sacred writings or another source

Two points, each developed, plus a named source applied to the question.

Name the source exactly (“The Bible teaches…”, “Qur’an 2:256 says…”, “Pope Francis said…”), then explain how it supports your point. If you forget the source you lose marks, however good the rest is.

12marks
Evaluate this statement

Arguments for, arguments against, and a justified conclusion. Plus 3 marks for spelling, punctuation and grammar.

Use PEEL for the argument that agrees. Then a However paragraph using PEEL for the other side. Finish with a judgement: which side is stronger, and why. You must use religious arguments; you may add non-religious ones.

A 12-mark answer, built with PEEL

“Divorce is never right.” Evaluate this statement.

PSome Christians agree that divorce is never right, because marriage is a sacred, lifelong promise made before God.

EJesus taught, “What God has joined together, let no one separate” (Mark 10:9), and the Catholic Church teaches that marriage is a sacrament that cannot be broken.

EThis means that ending a marriage breaks vows made to God as well as to a partner, so for these Christians it can never be right.

LTherefore they would agree with the statement.

Howeverother Christians, such as the Church of England, and many Muslims, disagree. Islam allows divorce as a last resort after attempts at reconciliation, and many Christians argue that compassion matters more than keeping a promise that causes suffering, for example in an abusive marriage. Jesus showed compassion to people whose lives had gone wrong.

JudgementOverall, I think the statement is too strong. The sanctity of vows is important, but forcing someone to stay in a harmful marriage goes against the more important teaching to love your neighbour, so divorce can sometimes be right.

Introduction to Christianity and the Bible

Christianity: Beliefs · Paper 1 · 8062/13

Key points

  • Christians follow the teachings of Jesus Christ, whom they believe is the Son of God.
  • The Bible has two parts: the Old Testament (shared with Judaism) and the New Testament (Jesus and the early Church).

Lesson 2

The nature of God and the Trinity

Christianity: Beliefs · Paper 1 · 8062/13

Key points

  • Omnipotent: God is all-powerful. He created the universe and can do anything.
  • Loving (omnibenevolent): God cares for every person and sent Jesus to save humanity.
  • Just: God is fair. He judges people and wants justice in the world.
  • The Trinity: one God in three Persons: Father (creator), Son (Jesus, God in human form) and Holy Spirit (God at work in the world today).
  • Each Person is fully God, but there is only one God. This is the oneness of God.

  • “For nothing will be impossible with God” (Luke 1:37)
  • “Go and make disciples of all nations, baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit” (Matthew 28:19)

Lesson 3

Creation: the Word and the Spirit

Christianity: Beliefs · Paper 1 · 8062/13

Key points

  • Genesis 1 says God created the universe in six days and rested on the seventh.
  • Literal Christians (creationists) believe this happened exactly as written.
  • Non-literal Christians accept the Big Bang and evolution, but believe God started and guided creation.
  • Genesis 1:1-3 shows the Spirit “hovering over the waters”: the Holy Spirit was present at creation.
  • John 1:1-3 calls Jesus the Word: “through him all things were made”. So the Son was also present.
  • This shows all three Persons of the Trinity were involved in creation.

Lesson 4

Sin and original sin

Christianity: Beliefs · Paper 1 · 8062/13

Key points

  • Sin is any thought or action that goes against God’s will, such as lying, stealing or hatred.
  • Original sin is the tendency to sin that all humans are born with.
  • It comes from Adam and Eve disobeying God by eating fruit from the tree of knowledge (Genesis 3). This is the Fall.
  • Sin separates humans from God. Christians believe Jesus came to repair this relationship.
  • Catholics believe baptism washes away original sin.

Sources to quote

Lesson 10

The problem of evil and suffering

Christianity: Beliefs · Paper 1 · 8062/13

Key points

  • Moral evil: suffering caused by humans. Natural evil: suffering caused by nature.
  • The problem: if God is omnipotent and loving, why does he allow evil and suffering?
  • Free will: God gave humans free will, so they can choose evil. Without it we would be robots.
  • Soul-making (Irenaeus): suffering helps people grow, develop courage and compassion.
  • Job: in the Bible, Job suffers but keeps his faith. Humans cannot understand all of God’s plan.
  • Christians respond practically by praying and helping those who suffer.

Sources to quote

Worship

Christianity: Practices · Paper 1 · 8062/13

Key points

  • Liturgical worship: a set order of service with set prayers, often led by a priest, e.g. Catholic Mass.
  • Non-liturgical worship: no fixed structure; may focus on Bible readings and a sermon, e.g. many Baptist services.

  • Private worship: praying or reading the Bible alone at home.
  • The Bible is used in all of these: readings, sermons and private study.

Lesson 4

Sacraments: baptism

Christianity: Practices · Paper 1 · 8062/13

Key points

  • A sacrament is an outward sign of an inward grace (a ceremony through which God’s grace is received).
  • Catholics and Orthodox recognise seven sacraments. Most Protestants recognise two: baptism and Eucharist. Quakers and the Salvation Army have none.
  • Infant baptism (Catholic, Anglican, Orthodox): a baby is baptised with water; parents and godparents make promises. It removes original sin and welcomes the child into the Church.
  • Believers’ baptism (Baptists, Pentecostals): adults choose to be baptised, usually by full immersion, after declaring their faith.
  • Baptists argue a baby cannot understand or choose faith.

Sources to quote

  • “Go and make disciples of all nations, baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit” (Matthew 28:19)
  • Jesus was baptised by John in the River Jordan (Matthew 3:13-17)

Lesson 5

Sacraments: the Eucharist (Holy Communion)

Christianity: Practices · Paper 1 · 8062/13

Key points

  • The Eucharist remembers the Last Supper, when Jesus shared bread and wine with his disciples.
  • Catholics: the bread and wine become the body and blood of Jesus (transubstantiation). It is called Mass.
  • Orthodox: the Divine Liturgy; Christ is truly present, but it is a mystery.
  • Many Protestants: the bread and wine are symbols. It is a memorial (remembering), e.g. the Lord’s Supper in Baptist churches.
  • It brings Christians closer to God and to each other.

Sources to quote

Pilgrimage: Lourdes and Iona

Christianity: Practices · Paper 1 · 8062/13

Key points

  • A pilgrimage is a journey to a holy place for religious reasons.
  • Lourdes (France): in 1858 Bernadette had visions of the Virgin Mary. Pilgrims bathe in the spring water, hoping for healing; many go to help sick people.

  • Some Christians (e.g. many Protestants) think pilgrimage is unnecessary, because God can be found anywhere.

Introduction to Islam: Sunni and Shi’a

Islam: Beliefs · Paper 1 · 8062/15

Key points

  • Islam began in Arabia in the 7th century CE. Muslims believe Allah revealed the Qur’an to the Prophet Muhammad, starting in 610 CE.
  • After Muhammad died in 632 CE, Muslims disagreed about who should lead.

  • Shi’a Muslims believe Muhammad chose his cousin and son-in-law Ali as leader.
  • Both share the core beliefs, but differ on leadership and some practices. Not in the specification, but key to every ‘different views’ answer.

Lesson 2

Tawhid and the nature of Allah

Islam: Beliefs · Paper 1 · 8062/15

Key points

  • Tawhid: the Oneness of God. Allah is one, unique and cannot be divided. This is the most important belief in Islam.
  • Shirk (giving Allah partners) is the greatest sin.
  • Omnipotence: all-powerful. Beneficence: all-good and generous. Mercy: all-forgiving and compassionate.
  • Fairness and justice: Allah judges fairly. For Shi’a Muslims, Adalat (God’s justice) is one of the five roots.
  • Immanence: Allah is close and involved in the world. Transcendence: Allah is beyond and above the universe.

Sources to quote

  • “Say, ‘He is God, the One, God the eternal. He begot no one nor was He begotten. No one is comparable to Him’” (Qur’an 112)
  • “We are closer to him than his jugular vein” (Qur’an 50:16)
  • Every surah but one begins: “In the name of God, the Lord of Mercy, the Giver of Mercy”

Islam: Beliefs · Paper 1 · 8062/15

Lesson 5

Wealth: right attitudes and uses

Theme F: Human rights and social justice · Paper 2A

Key points

  • Wealth is not wrong in itself, but greed and the love of money are.
  • Christianity: wealth should be used to help others; it is hard for the rich to enter God’s kingdom if they love money.
  • Islam: wealth is a gift from Allah, held in trust; Muslims must give zakah and avoid hoarding.
  • Good uses of wealth: supporting family, charity, fair business.

Sources to quote

Lesson 6

Poverty and charity

Theme F: Human rights and social justice · Paper 2A

Key points

  • Duty to tackle poverty: both religions teach helping the poor and tackling the causes of poverty (e.g. unfair trade, debt).
  • Responsibilities of those in poverty: to work if they can and help themselves where possible.
  • Charity: giving money or time. Some worry giving money directly may be misused; others say help should be given freely.
  • Christianity: tithing (giving 10%); charities such as Christian Aid. Islam: zakah and sadaqah.

Lesson 7

Exploitation of the poor

Theme F: Human rights and social justice · Paper 2A

Key points

  • Fair pay: workers should be paid enough to live on (e.g. the Real Living Wage).
  • Excessive interest on loans: payday loans trap people in debt. Islam forbids interest (riba), so Islamic banks avoid it.
  • People-trafficking: moving people illegally to exploit them for work or sex. The Modern Slavery Act 2015 tackles it.
  • Christians and Muslims condemn exploitation because every person has God-given worth.

Sources to quote

  • “Do not take advantage of a hired worker who is poor and needy” (Deuteronomy 24:14)
